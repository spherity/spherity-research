    title: A Global Cooperation Model for Sovereign Industrial AI
    subtitle: European dataspaces and open standards for verifiable evidence across platforms
    version: "1.0"
    date: "2026-11-01"
    description: "Industrial AI needs infrastructure that connects evidence across companies and jurisdictions at operational speed. European dataspaces are a first step in participant-controlled cooperation, not a demonstrated infrastructure foundation for IPCEI-AI. Dataspace concepts and policy precede today's LLM, frontier and agentic AI wave; existing governance and exchange mechanisms do not establish fitness for high throughput, low latency and large data volumes. We argue for fast-track modernisation that meets workload-specific performance requirements without relaxing access control, verifiability or participant authority. Substantial engineering and validation remain.\r

      \r

      Palantir's operational ontology provides a demanding architectural reference for connecting data, meaning, logic and governed actions. Our proposed counter-model extends that operational ambition across independently governed implementations, with credible provider choice. Its foundation is access-controlled, verifiable evidence graphs built on open international standards and industry-agreed semantics. These graphs connect decision-relevant claims and provenance while bulk data can remain in specialist systems.\r

      \r

      We combine primary-source review, platform economics, comparable architecture patterns and a proposed industrial experiment. The resulting agenda joins efficient data movement, task-specific graph views, scoped authorization and maintained evidence status. Europe can lead this modernisation through global coordination with US innovators, partners across Asia-Pacific including China, and other participant-controlled ecosystems. Joint vocabulary governance, implementation profiles and reciprocal testing must turn shared principles into working interoperability. This is a collaborative blueprint for global digital sovereignty and a competitive multipolar economy, not a claim of production readiness. Performance, assurance, resilience and economic gains remain outcomes to demonstrate together. Accelerated investment should fund redesign and testing where needed, rather than assume that existing dataspace implementations already satisfy AI-first requirements."

questions_answered:
  - question: What is Europe’s answer to Palantir?
    answer: This research proposes industrial cooperation across independently governed platforms, using shared semantics, access control and verifiable evidence. Europe’s dataspace experience can help convene that effort globally. It is a design and modernization agenda, not the announcement of a completed European substitute.
  - question: Does Palantir only work inside one company?
    answer: No. Palantir documents multi-organization ecosystems. The comparison concerns platform-centred integration versus cooperation across independently governed implementations. It does not assert a universal closed-world semantics or a single-company limit.
  - question: Why does the ontology matter for AI?
    answer: An ontology makes relevant concepts and relationships explicit. A graph can connect a specific component, configuration, report and decision rule so software and people can inspect the evidence. The result can improve grounding and repeatability, provided the mappings, sources and controls are maintained.
  - question: Do knowledge graphs eliminate hallucinations or always make AI faster?
    answer: No. They can reduce irrelevant context and help check support for claims. Incorrect links, weak sources or unsupported model explanations remain possible. Measure the complete workflow, including graph construction, retrieval and verification costs.
  - question: Does open-world semantics mean open access to company data?
    answer: No. Open-world semantics treats knowledge as potentially incomplete. Access control decides who may discover or use particular records, relationships and results. A shared vocabulary does not grant permission to read the data that use it.
  - question: Are today’s dataspaces already ready for AI-first infrastructure?
    answer: That is not established by this research. Current components need workload-specific tests for throughput, latency, volume, evidence freshness and authorization. The papers argue for fast-track modernisation that meets performance and trust requirements together.
  - question: What does the robot graph demonstrate?
    answer: It shows how fictional evidence can support a configured product and how a withdrawn report can trigger reassessment of dependent decisions. It does not verify signatures, certify a robot or decide whether a regulation applies.
  - question: Can one graph automate compliance with every regulation?
    answer: Selected evidence can be reused across assessments, but each has its own applicability, authority, evidence and decision criteria. The demonstration separates seven assessment lenses and does not produce a universal compliance approval.
  - question: How can companies participate?
    answer: Bring a bounded cross-company decision, the relevant participants and a willingness to agree vocabulary, evidence rules and open tests. Spherity invites first movers to test useful cooperation and provider substitution against measurable outcomes.

<h2 id="strategic-answer">What is Europe’s answer to Palantir?</h2>
<p>Palantir shows the power of connecting data, business meaning, logic and governed action through an operational ontology. Europe’s strategic opportunity is to make comparable cooperation work across independently governed companies and platforms. This research proposes <strong>access-controlled, verifiable evidence graphs built on open international standards and industry-agreed semantics</strong>. Participants prepare their own information, exchange permitted evidence and retain authority over consequential actions. Europe can lead this effort with partners in the US, Asia-Pacific and other regions. Existing dataspaces are a first step: fast-track modernisation and demanding operational tests are still needed for AI-first use.</p>
<p>Sovereignty means the practical capacity to choose partners, govern permitted uses and preserve essential operations. Palantir and other capable providers can participate. Shared vocabulary, discovery and trust operators can also become concentration points: credible substitution and continuity need testing.</p>

<h2 id="why-ontology-matters">From records to meaning, evidence and governed decisions</h2>
<ul>
<li>A <strong>knowledge graph</strong> connects identifiable things and claims using labelled relationships: this component belongs to this configuration; this report concerns this test.</li>
<li>An <strong>evidence graph</strong> adds the supporting records, origin, interpretation, status and conditions under which a claim can be used.</li>
<li>An <strong>assurance graph</strong> connects claims, evidence and reasoning to a bounded assurance argument. A signature establishes specific integrity/origin properties when verified; it does not establish physical truth, safety or legal acceptance.</li>
</ul>
<p>Well-maintained graphs can help retrieve the relevant facts, preserve provenance, reuse explicit checks and show which decisions depend on changing evidence. This can reduce repeated document extraction, irrelevant prompt context and unsupported answers. Whether it reduces tokens, latency, cost or risk must be measured on the actual task, including graph maintenance, distributed retrieval and verification overhead. Graphs are not required for every AI task and do not eliminate hallucinations.</p>
<p>Open-world semantics allows knowledge to be incomplete: an unavailable report is not proof that it never existed or that the product failed. A bounded operational rule can still require accepted evidence before authorizing action. <strong>Open semantics does not mean public data or unrestricted access.</strong></p>

<h2 id="modernising-dataspaces">Fast-track modernisation for AI-first workloads</h2>
<p>Europe’s dataspace work is a foundation for coordination and experimentation. It is not evidence that current implementations already satisfy IPCEI-AI or frontier-AI throughput, latency and volume requirements. The goal is <strong>fast-track modernisation without relaxing access control, verifiability or participant authority</strong>. Retain components that pass defined tests.</p>
<p>The meta-platform argument is participant-controlled cooperation across platforms. It does not imply a proven exponential network-value law, guaranteed GDP uplift or automatic efficiency gains from decentralization. Shared vocabulary, discovery or trust operators can themselves become concentration points; credible substitution and continuity need testing.</p>
<p>A <strong>Participant Exchange Gateway</strong> manages permitted exchanges. A dataspace connector built with Eclipse Dataspace Components is one implementation option for its exchange functions, complemented by identity, evidence verification and action authorization. <strong>Shared Meaning</strong> provides semantic and vocabulary-hub functions. These are logical shared roles, potentially with several providers. Every participant connects to Shared Meaning, Discovery and Trust/status.</p>
<p>ODRL is a policy-expression standard used in dataspace implementations, and EDC provides a reference implementation of policy evaluation. Neither ODRL nor a connector alone is a universal, complete enforcement or legal-recognition mechanism. Shared vocabularies, catalogues, resolvers and trust metadata also need authenticated publication, integrity checks, scoped access and explicit freshness bounds. Cached or ledger-backed information does not remove freshness or accepted-authority decisions.</p>
<h2 id="global-cooperation">Europe as a global convener</h2>
<p>Europe can turn its experience in industrial dataspaces into a globally collaborative offer. The next step is shared implementation profiles, joint testing and accountable recognition arrangements with international partners. W3C work, UNECE traceability initiatives, US innovation and Asia-Pacific programmes provide relevant cooperation channels. Technical interoperability, accepted issuer authority and legal recognition are separate achievements; a technical connection does not establish all three.</p>

<h2 id="practical-guide">Build a bounded first-mover experiment</h2>
<p>Start with a decision that crosses real organizational boundaries: accepting a component, reviewing a changed test report or assembling a release dossier. Agree the relevant identifiers, vocabulary, evidence requirements and responsibilities. Keep each participant’s source processing and action authority local. Exchange only what the task permits. Then test the complete workflow, including missing permission, stale evidence, conflicting claims and service failure.</p>
<p>Measure correctness and unsupported claims, end-to-end response time, tokens and total cost, evidence freshness, recovery and provider substitution. Include the cost imposed on suppliers and laboratories. Demonstrate that a replaceable provider can change without silently losing meaning, authority, evidence history or required behavior. These tests should guide fast-track dataspace modernisation and procurement.</p>
<p><strong>Build the first interoperable evidence workflow with us.</strong> We invite manufacturers, suppliers, laboratories, platform providers, standards communities and public-sector partners to agree one useful decision, a shared vocabulary and an open test protocol. Bring a real use case and help demonstrate cooperation across providers. Collaborative leadership matters because no single company can establish an ecosystem’s meaning, trust and operating rules alone.</p>
