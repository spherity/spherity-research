answer_summary: DPP requirements are developing through different legal, standards and implementation pathways. Shared semantics, verifiable evidence and business identity can connect them, but data transfer does not by itself establish equivalent meaning, authority or legal acceptance. This paper combines a source-linked evidence atlas with a proposed infrastructure for physical products, virtual products and physical AI.

key_takeaways:
  - Distinguish a legal obligation, a published specification, a pilot and an independently demonstrated exchange. Each answers a different question.
  - Separate fixed application dates from planned adoption windows and conditional requirements. A registry launch does not establish every operational capability.
  - AAS has international standards and programme support. Coexistence, mapping and displacement in particular applications remain different scenarios.
  - The proposed evidence infrastructure combines product claims, actor authority and enforced permission. Its integrated benefits require bounded tests and independent review.

questions_answered:
  - question: What is the difference between the paper and the Atlas?
    answer: The 63-page version 1.0 whitepaper is a fixed manuscript prepared on 25 October 2026. Its Figure 1 and baseline counts refer to frozen dataset 0.1.0, dated 23 October. The interactive Atlas uses research release 0.2.0, which preserves separately documented updates and correction history. Its technical seal fixes a reproducible snapshot; independent qualified review remains pending. Its current counts should not be substituted into the paper’s frozen results.
  - question: Do the map colours show whether every product needs a passport?
    answer: "No. Colours summarise the phase of a specifically evidenced measure: applying sector duties, an adopted framework with future or conditional rollout, a proposal, or early public activity. Sector, territory, dates and exceptions remain essential. Grey means unassessed or excluded by the current filters."
  - question: Are the EU product-measure dates compliance deadlines?
    answer: Only where the cited law establishes an application date. Most ESPR work-plan dates are indicative adoption targets. Construction passport timing depends on a delegated act and statutory transitions. Machinery and Cyber Resilience Act milestones are adjacent obligations, not additional DPP mandates.
  - question: Does a connection in the graph prove interoperability?
    answer: No. A connection may document participation, a reference, a version-specific dependency or a reported result. The paper’s Japan–Catena-X example is a bounded consortium-reported demonstration, not an independently reproduced finding of worldwide compatibility or legal recognition.
  - question: Does the research predict that AAS will disappear?
    answer: No. It recognises international AAS standards, programmes and prototypes. AAS may remain an industrial source model, connect through explicit mappings, or be displaced in particular uses. These scenarios need comparable implementation evidence; AI acceleration does not establish one inevitable outcome.
  - question: Has the whitepaper or dataset been independently reviewed?
    answer: The manuscript is a final author version, not a peer-reviewed publication. Automated consistency checks and machine-assisted source checks do not constitute independent qualified review. The Atlas preserves source-access limits and review status, and the paper specifies the further legal review and experiments required.

<p class="dpp-thesis-kicker">The paper’s architectural proposition</p>
<h2 id="dpp-thesis-heading">A shared evidence infrastructure for DPPs and AI</h2>
<blockquote><p>Access-controlled, verifiable evidence graphs for physical products, virtual products and physical AI—built on open international standards and open-world semantics.</p></blockquote>
<p class="dpp-thesis-outlook">Connect evidence about a product to its source, meaning and version, and connect each requested operation to an actor’s authority and current permission. This is a proposition for shared infrastructure. Its effectiveness, interoperability and institutional acceptance require separate tests.</p>

<h2 id="registry-and-semantics">Registration is one part of the evidence infrastructure</h2>
<p>The EU registry’s statutory functions include registration information and a semantic repository. These are distinct from a discovery catalogue, a federated semantic hub and the systems hosting detailed product evidence. A registration proof does not establish that every underlying claim is correct.</p>
<p>The paper separates the registry’s legal deadline and launch announcement from the capabilities documented in its September 2026 user guide. It did not perform an authenticated registration or API test. The lower part of this figure is the author’s proposed architecture, rather than an observed common deployment.</p>

<h2 id="wallets-and-permissions">Connect evidence, authority and permitted operations</h2>
<p>Product evidence concerns an object; business identity and mandates concern the actor requesting an operation. The proposed architecture combines these with scoped recognition, an agreed policy vocabulary and enforcement at the point of access and use.</p>
<p>W3C Recommendations, experimental Working Drafts, UNTP pilot editions and rolling guidance have different status. Wallet use is not universal across UNTP implementations. ODRL expresses permissions and constraints; it does not enforce them by itself. A supplier’s policy cannot override lawful statutory access, and an authority credential does not confer unlimited access to every record.</p>

Those are distinct adoption signals. It may remain a useful industrial source model, connect through explicit mappings, or be displaced in particular applications. <h2 id="research-question">The question behind the research</h2>
<p>How do fragmented DPP requirements connect through shared semantics, verifiable evidence and business identity—and where does interoperability remain unproven?</p>
<p>The practical approach begins with one product and one consequential decision. Identify the applicable requirement, actor, scope, event date and recipient’s evidence needs. Preserve useful source systems and explicit mappings. Then test legitimate and adverse cases with a second implementation, keeping legal recognition and operational benefit as separate questions.</p>
